Ostrovok: hotel & apartments page

Time
~30–35 min
Role
Design lead: research, strategy, UI, specs
Result
+7% bookings · +$1.8M/year

A redesign of the most expensive page in the funnel. The hotel page is where people decide whether to pay. It hadn't changed in years, and apartments, a fast-growing share of inventory, were squeezed into a template built for hotels.

My role and ownership

ownership

What I owned

  1. Solo designer on the whole project.
  2. Backlog prioritisation based on funnel data.
  3. Interview guide and running 14 interviews.
  4. Shipping in parts: a separate A/B test for every block.
  5. The call to remove the selection block for apartments, built on our own extranet.
  6. Handover to engineering.

Team

  1. PM — metrics, funnel analytics.
  2. Analyst for A/B tests.

Context and problem

~4 min · Why now

Ostrovok is a brand of Emerging Travel Group. After Booking.com left Russia in 2022, traffic surged: the company assembled a product team of big-tech alumni, and I joined to lead design. The hotel page, the busiest point of the funnel, had grown outdated, and there had never been enough engineering resource to rethink it end to end.

By the end of the year the page team had capacity freed up for the coming quarters: either we enter that window with a ready redesign, or the page stays as is indefinitely. The main trigger was apartments, a growing segment the page was never designed for. Still, every block was designed to be reusable: one system for both hotels and apartments.

There were no user complaints. There was an opportunity to move conversion, and we had to find the growth points ourselves.

Constraints

  1. Hit the window. Design finished and approved before the holidays, otherwise the window closes.
  2. Don't hurt conversion. The highest-revenue point of the funnel, hence shipping in parts with a separate A/B test for every block.
  3. Improve the UX and lift the metrics. Conversion to the next funnel steps and, ultimately, to bookings.

Why this first

~2 min · Prioritisation

“Redo the hotel page” isn't a problem statement. We pulled session recordings and events in PostHog and looked at where exactly sessions ended. In 74% of cases the last block was rate selection: people got to the rooms and never made it to the booking form.

So the first bet was the rooms and rates block. The header, the gallery and the apartments flow grew out of the same research.

Research

~5 min · 14 interviews

One body of data for the whole project. I wrote the guide, and we ran 14 customer development interviews with people who travel regularly, but are neither Ostrovok users nor employees.

The guide moved from a step-by-step walkthrough of their last real trip (CJM) to general patterns: selection criteria, behaviour on search results and the property page, expectations for photos, reasons to drop off.

Synthesis on the board turned into a backlog of changes, and funnel analytics set the priorities: the biggest losses were at the rate selection step. Order of work: rates first, then the header, the gallery and the remaining blocks.

Insights that changed the design

“What am I choosing here?”

Apartment guests facing a rates block with a single option. They read the step as a breakage. Hence the call to remove the block entirely.

Respondent · apartments → Apartments: remove the block

Apartments are chosen by photos, and the gallery is too small

People choose a place with their eyes, but the photos were hard to make out: people opened fullscreen right away. For apartments, the gallery is the main selection tool.

Pattern · apartments → Header & gallery

They look for the listed amenities in the photos

A frequent task for respondents: find in the photos what the amenities list says, and check it. They jumped between blocks for that. Hence amenities inside the expanded gallery.

Rooms & rates (hotels)

~7 min · +5%

Problem
The interviews showed rate selection was the most confusing step. Rates were laid out as rows, conditions as text in composite cells. To spot the differences you had to read every row in full: what was identical and what was different looked the same. Some people discovered only after booking that the “included breakfast” wasn't there.
Solution
Choosing a rate is a search for differences. Rates became vertical cards side by side: every condition gets its own slot, aligned across the cards, so one glance compares one parameter across all rates; meaningful differences are highlighted and visible before reading; room photos and amenities moved to a column on the left, leaving only the rates on the right.
Result
+5%

Apartments: remove the block

~4 min · +9% · strongest test

Problem
In an apartment there is no room to choose: the property is the room. All that remains is cancellation and payment, and the hotel-style “room + rate” block is overkill for that. In the interviews people recalled this step as a breakage: “what am I choosing here?”. They got lost among the rates and couldn't tell whether it was the same apartment: the photos in the block seemed to match the ones above on the page, but not all of them did.
Solution
The selection block was removed entirely. Rate conditions became toggles inside a single sticky booking block with the price and the button. This was possible thanks to our own extranet: apartments come directly from hosts, and we control the rate structure ourselves.
Result
+9% strongest test

How I got to this hypothesis

01 · Signal

In PostHog I watched session recordings where people dropped off at the rates block. Apartments looked different: people weren't comparing, they were looking for what they were even supposed to choose, and then left. The backlog task said “redraw the cards”, but I decided to first ask whether a choice was needed at all.

02 · Data

I went to the analysts and asked for the distribution of rates across apartments. The median property has one rate, around 90% of properties. So for most of them the selection block was an empty step.

03 · Opportunity

Apartments live in our own extranet (unlike hotels, which come in different formats from dozens of suppliers). Rates there are structured and driven by checkboxes: cancellation, payment and so on, all in one card. So the conditions could become toggles right in the booking block.

Header & gallery

~2 min · +2.9%

Problem
People choose a place to stay with their eyes, yet the gallery kept getting criticised in the interviews: the photos were too small to make anything out, so you had to open the fullscreen view right away.
Solution
We freed up space for the gallery: cleaned up the UI and moved the reviews-and-rating carousel lower, so the photos got noticeably bigger. Amenities moved inside the expanded gallery: respondents often hunted for an apartment's features in the photos and checked them against the amenities list, jumping between blocks. Now the photos and the list sit side by side.
Result
+2.9%

Takeaways

  1. A resource window is a legitimate reason for a project: it started with an opportunity, and analytics plus research found where to aim it.
  2. One research corpus for the whole project: 14 interviews were enough for both the rates and the gallery.
  3. Sometimes the best solution is to remove a block, not improve it: on apartments this delivered more than the redesigned comparison did on hotels.

Tinkoff Kassa: payment form

Time
~8–10 min
Scale
30M+ MAU
Scope
Frontend only
Result
+9% card · +16% Tinkoff Pay

The form every business on Tinkoff Kassa shows when customers pay for its goods and services. Small changes, huge reach, and constraints from partners, businesses and a backend we couldn't touch.

My role and ownership

ownership

What I owned

  1. Concept and flow variants.
  2. Form UI.
  3. Working within partner constraints.
  4. Grooming with engineering.

Team

  1. PM — help with analytics.
  2. Design director — design stakeholder.

Context

~1 min

The payment form is a Tinkoff b2c product that 30M+ users visit every month to pay for their online purchases.

Problems

~1 min

  1. Old UI.
  2. High cognitive load due to the many heavy buttons of alternative payment methods.
  3. Low conversion to payment via Tinkoff Pay.

Support ticket analysis

~1 min · Insights

  1. Users enter their phone number in the card field.
  2. Users click the payment button without entering a card.

→ Insights

Task

~1 min

Make the UI up to date and, at the same time, increase conversion to card and Tinkoff Pay payments.

Constraints

~1 min

  1. Frontend only. No changes to the backend or flows.
  2. Partner guidelines for alternative payment methods.

Process

~2 min

We collected references, analysed competitors, made hypotheses and sketched out many different approaches to the flow.

During grooming with engineering we found out we were very limited in critical changes: the form supports merchant customisation, which a large number of users rely on. So we moved iteratively: the first stage of the redesign, without major changes in functionality.

Solution

~3 min

  1. Less cognitive load: lighter buttons and a maximum of three alternative payment methods on screen.
  2. Text in blocks that makes the interface easier to understand.
  1. Same layout for web and mobile, for easier development and support.
  1. More room to show the benefits and convenience of Tinkoff Pay.
  1. For fans of the dark theme.
